# Rights, Claims, and Disclosure Gate

Use this reference before producing or publishing work involving real people, brands, logos, copyrighted designs, recognizable private property, product claims, political themes, or fictional announcements.

## Contents

1. Status register
2. Decision gate
3. Real-person likeness
4. Brands and products
5. Cultural authenticity
6. Disclosure patterns

## 1. Status register

Record each controlled element with:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Asset ID | Stable reference such as `CHAR-01` or `PROD-01` |
| Rights type | Likeness, trademark, copyrighted design, music, location, claim, or other |
| Status | `owned`, `approved`, `licensed`, `public-domain`, `not-required`, `pending`, or `unknown` |
| Scope | Internal concept, portfolio, paid social, broadcast, geography, duration |
| Evidence | Approval, license, contract, source, or owner statement |
| Required disclosure | Exact copy and placement |

Do not convert `pending` or `unknown` into implied permission.

## 2. Decision gate

- **Internal exploration:** Proceed with clear internal-only labeling and no distribution claim. Keep rights unresolved in the package.
- **Portfolio or case study:** Confirm usage rights and label fictional or unofficial concepts prominently.
- **Commercial distribution:** Require confirmed authorization for likeness, brand assets, music, footage, and material claims within the intended scope.
- **News-like announcement:** Verify the underlying event. Never frame speculation as fact or mimic an official announcement without authorization.

If permission is unavailable, use a fictional person, generic unbranded product, original wardrobe, licensed assets, or an abstract treatment.

## 3. Real-person likeness

Document consent and intended usage. Preserve dignity and avoid false endorsement, fabricated statements, sexualized treatment, deceptive context, or reputational harm. Do not rely on public visibility as proof of commercial permission.

For a speculative sports transfer or celebrity concept:

- label it as fictional and unofficial;
- avoid suggesting the club, player, sponsor, or league approved it;
- do not fabricate quotes, press events, or contracts;
- remove or replace the real likeness if the output will be commercialized without appropriate rights.

## 4. Brands, products, and claims

Lock supplied brand geometry and palette, but plan compositing for exact marks and text. Verify claims such as “natural,” “organic,” “pure,” health benefits, performance, origin, awards, or certifications before placing them in copy.

Keep the approval record separate from creative taste. A beautiful generated package is not proof that the trademark, label, music, or claim is cleared.

## 5. Cultural authenticity

Ground authenticity in evidence:

- real locations and architecture;
- documented clothing, signage, language, rituals, and behavior;
- locally plausible casting from approved references;
- natural diversity in age, appearance, and social context;
- review by a knowledgeable local stakeholder when the portrayal is material.

Do not instruct a model to produce stereotyped “facial features” for a nationality or region. Describe the actual setting, casting references, wardrobe, and behavior instead.

## 6. Disclosure patterns

Choose language that cannot be mistaken for official status:

- `Fictional AI concept. Not an official announcement or endorsement.`
- `Unofficial speculative case study created for creative demonstration.`
- `AI-generated visualization; final product appearance and claims require brand approval.`

Place the disclosure where viewers can reasonably see it: title card, caption, description, and case-study cover when needed. Do not hide a material disclosure in small end-frame copy alone.

Before handoff, report:

1. what is cleared;
2. what remains unresolved;
3. what disclosure is required;
4. what must be replaced if approval is not obtained.
